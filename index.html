Mar – Dec 2024

Who can afford to wait: off-farm income and the price a household gets

Team leader · National Undergraduate Innovation & Entrepreneurship Program, Renmin University

When a household has no realistic alternative to the buyer at its gate, what does that cost it?

Food-security policy treats the farm gate as a price. I wanted to look at it as a relationship: who a household can sell to, what it knows about prices elsewhere, and whether walking away is an option at all.

I built a panel covering 680 agricultural counties and ran more than twenty field interviews across them, then modeled selling behavior with endowment effects and hyperbolic discounting — because the pattern in the interviews wasn't one of farmers misreading prices, but of decisions made under constraints that make waiting expensive.

The hard part was measurement. Bargaining power has no column in any dataset, so what this project contributes is a design. I paired county-level panel data with interviews to work out which observable conditions stand in for a household's real room to negotiate, and modeled the sale decision so that waiting carries a cost.

The clearest result was about liquidity. Households with a member earning wages away from home could afford to wait, and at the farm gate waiting is most of what bargaining power amounts to. Off-farm income loosens the cash constraint that forces a sale at harvest, and the buyer's leverage falls with it. The conclusion I would defend in an interview is that the policy worth paying for is whatever makes waiting affordable, above all storage and short-term credit.

When "pay later" backfires: instalments, impulse buying, and returns

Co-leader · National Undergraduate Innovation and Entrepreneurship Program, Renmin University

Does splitting a price into instalments change what people buy, or only when they pay for it?

Our hypothesis came from the endowment effect: if you hold the goods before you pay for them, you value them more, so you should send fewer of them back. We tested it on a survey of just over two hundred consumers, of whom about eighty-five entered the regressions, and paired it with semi-structured interviews on the merchant side, which almost nobody had looked at.

The result ran the other way. People who used instalment payment more often returned more and bought on impulse considerably more. Two readings fit. The service may be serving as a free trial, returned before the window closes, or the impulse purchase may simply meet a cooling-off period once the box arrives. Either way the endowment effect is not doing the work our design assumed, and that is the more interesting finding. Use is concentrated among 18-to-25-year-olds; older consumers use it significantly less. Merchants told us conversion and basket size rise in the short run while their cash flow becomes harder to predict.

The sample is small and the fit is modest, so these are directions rather than settled magnitudes.

Urban–rural integration: land-use reform and rural service delivery

Independent research · School of Economics, Renmin University

If public services follow land rather than people, who gets left out — and what does land reform actually move?

Land is still what most rural livelihoods rest on, and a great many households rest on it. Changing who may use it, and how, is never only a question about land.

Integration is usually measured by how much land changes use. I wanted to know what happens next, whether provision moves when land does. I led a comparative policy analysis with a Liaoning case study and co-authored a report submitted to local governments.

The answer is a negative one, which is worth reporting because policy assumes the opposite. Land moved and services did not follow. Reform changes what a household may do with its land well before it changes what that household can actually get.

May 2025

Urban–rural integration in Northeast China

Independent research · School of Economics, Renmin University

Why does integration stall hardest in the places that need it most?

The three northeastern provinces get discussed as one region with one problem, an old industrial base in decline. I compared how integration is actually being attempted across Heilongjiang, Jilin and Liaoning, and built a typology from the cases: a regional agricultural brand in Suihua, a company town built around the auto industry outside Changchun, an open-pit mine and a steel works in Liaoning being turned into something a city can live beside.

The unevenness repeats at every scale. Heilongjiang trails Jilin and Liaoning; inside each province, the capital and the large cities do well and the small and peripheral ones do not. Around the strongest cities I found polarization where the policy expects radiation, with the core absorbing the factors it was meant to send outward. A growth pole expected to carry its hinterland is therefore the wrong instrument here. The places furthest out need direct fiscal transfer and factor mobility that runs both ways.

Fall 2025

From fugitive to rebellion: Black education during and after segregation

Course paper · African American and African Studies 130, UC Davis

Segregated Black schools and the Independent Black Institutions that followed Brown are usually told as a single story of loss and recovery. I compared them along three axes: what each was trying to produce in a child, how it taught, and who controlled it. Culturally responsive teaching survived the transition but changed form, from a covert survival strategy inside a hostile curriculum into an explicit political claim. The paper ends on something I still can't settle, which is that self-determination protects a community's children and can also close them off.

Fall 2025

Reading For Each and Every Child against its own evidence

Policy report analysis · African American and African Studies 130, UC Davis

The 2013 Equity and Excellence Commission report diagnoses opportunity gaps in K–12 education. I traced the three mechanisms it names, school finance tied to local property tax, a stratified teacher labor market and community conditions, and asked why formally race-neutral rules keep reproducing the same sorting. The share of Black teachers fell from roughly a third of the profession in the mid-twentieth century to under seven percent. That was a policy outcome rather than a side effect, and it shows up in student results.
